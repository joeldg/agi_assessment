Jobs watch

Thirteen testable claims about the move to a post-AGI economy, and where the evidence puts each one today. 1 Established · 5 Emerging · 7 No clear sign.

Capital is forward-looking: firms and markets act on what they expect AI to do before it shows in employment data, so their positioning is the earliest evidence. Each claim has written marks that decide its status, and its status moves at most one step a week; a move to Established or Contradicted is only proposed until the owner confirms it. J0 is the null hypothesis, that so far this is a normal technology transition, and every edition reports the strongest evidence for it. The thesis's end state, demand that depends on people without wages, is framing here; J9 measures the part of it that can be measured now.

Jobs is a separate tracker of the move to a post-AGI economy. It never feeds the Hidden AGI Index, the odds or the fire alarm, and it is not investment advice.

How to read a status

  • Established: Supported for two quarters, visible in official statistics and not only private data, with no live alternative explanation.
  • Supported: Two independent indicators of different kinds agree (for example an official series and company disclosures), sustained across two releases.
  • Emerging: One primary indicator moves the claim's way beyond its pre-2022 range or its comparison group, with the confounders examined and found insufficient.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: The main indicators move against the claim in at least two independent sources, sustained across two releases.

The claims

J0 · The null: so far, a normal technology transition

Emerging

So far, AI's effect on work stays within the range of past technology transitions: aggregate employment, wages and the pace of occupational change look like earlier waves, and gaps between AI-exposed and less-exposed work are small or explained by other causes.

Why we track it: the tracker's control. Without it, the tracker would only find what it looks for. Every edition reports the week's strongest evidence for it.

Marks

  • Established: Supported for two quarters in official data.
  • Supported: two sources of different kinds agree, across two releases.
  • Emerging: one primary source with at least twelve months of post-2022 data finds no differential effect beyond past waves.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: AI-exposed work diverges from less-exposed work beyond past-wave ranges in two independent official-data sources across two releases.

Indicators

  • Pace of occupational change against past tech waves. The Budget Lab at Yale, Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market (occupational churn, AI metrics, exposed vs unexposed workers; built on CPS microdata) · roughly monthly, after each CPS release · official data. Confounders: survey noise; the pandemic's distortions; falling CPS response rates. Compared with: the 1984-89 computer and 1996-2002 internet waves, with 2016-19 as a control period.
  • Prime-age (25-54) employment-population ratio. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Population Survey (seasonally adjusted) · monthly · official data. Confounders: the business cycle; falling CPS response rates. Compared with: its range in past expansions (2000-04 peak 81.9, 2020-01 peak 80.6).
  • Unemployment in AI-exposed against less-exposed occupations. CPS-based Federal Reserve research: St. Louis Fed, On the Economy, 'Is AI Contributing to Rising Unemployment? Evidence from Occupational Variation' (Ozkan and Sullivan, 2025-08-26); Richmond Fed Economic Briefs 26-26 and 26-27 on job-finding rates by AI exposure (Borovickova and Macaluso, Aug 2026, https://www.richmondfed.org/publications/research/economic_brief/2026/eb_26-26); the overall unemployment rate from BLS as the backdrop · irregular · official data. Confounders: exposure measures differ between studies; the rate cycle; correlation, not causation. Compared with: less AI-exposed occupations.
  • Labor productivity growth, nonfarm business (output per hour). U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Productivity and Costs (nonfarm business sector) · quarterly · official data. Confounders: the business cycle; revisions; AI capex itself lifting measured output. Compared with: the pre-2022 trend (OPHNFB 1.5%/yr 2007Q4-2019Q4) and the 1995-2004 IT-era pickup (3.1%/yr 1995Q4-2004Q4); a break far above trend cuts against the null.
  • Wage growth by occupation and industry. Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Wage Growth Tracker (median 12-month hourly wage growth of the same individuals, CPS microdata) · monthly, usually by the second Friday · official data. Confounders: inflation and the business cycle; falling CPS response rates; broad occupation and industry groups. Compared with: Professional and management, and Finance and business services, against Service, and Leisure and hospitality.

History

  • 2026-10-06 Start → Emerging: The Budget Lab at Yale, using CPS data through August 2026, finds the occupational mix not yet changing in ways that clearly align with AI and no clear AI footprint in its synthetic difference-in-differences analysis, which meets J0's Emerging mark: "one primary source with at least twelve months of post-2022 data finds no differential effect beyond past waves." Official aggregates agree: prime-age employment was 80.7% in September 2026, above its January 2020 level; unemployment, 4.2%, is inside its 2015-2019 range; Atlanta Fed wage-growth gaps sit inside their 2015-2019 ranges; and productivity growth since late 2022 lies between the pre-2022 trend and the IT-era pickup. Supported ("two sources of different kinds agree, across two releases.") is not met: these sources all rest on the CPS, and J0's own exposure indicator points the other way. St. Louis Fed economists find a 0.47 correlation between AI exposure and rising unemployment across occupations in 2022-2025, and Richmond Fed economists find that job-finding has fallen most for unemployed workers from highly exposed occupations since 2023, a component they call unprecedented outside the spread of personal computers. Contradicted is not met because Richmond places the gap within that precedent. J3 also starts at Emerging; the two fit because the young-worker gap falls on a small group (Dallas Fed economists estimate at most 0.1 point on the unemployment rate), though that estimate does not cover the Richmond job-finding gap or the 12.1% fall in Information payrolls since November 2022.

J1 · Capital is already pricing in labor substitution

No clear sign

Investors are already rewarding companies for replacing labor with AI and marking down businesses whose value rests on selling human labor, before the effects show in employment data.

Why we track it: the core of the section. Markets are forward-looking; their positioning is the earliest evidence of what they expect AI to do to work.

Marks

  • Established: Supported for two quarters, with capital allocation (venture share, capex) moving the same way.
  • Supported: two kinds agree across two releases.
  • Emerging: one primary indicator, either the basket derating against the comparison beyond what its earnings changes explain, or positive market reactions to AI-linked headcount cuts at several named firms within a quarter.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: the basket re-rates against the comparison and AI-linked headcount cuts draw negative reactions, in two sources across two releases.

Indicators

  • Labor-priced industries' share prices against the broad market: Nasdaq US Benchmark Computer Services, Professional Business Support Services, and Business Training and Employment Agencies indexes, each against the Nasdaq US Benchmark Index. Nasdaq Global Indexes, Nasdaq US Benchmark industry indexes, via FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis) · daily · official data. Confounders: interest rates and sector rotation; earnings changes unrelated to AI; index membership changes. Compared with: the Nasdaq US Benchmark Index (fred:NASDAQNQUSB) and each index's own pre-2022 range against it.
  • S&P 500 earnings calls citing "AI" (count and share of calls). FactSet Insight, Earnings Insight (John Butters): quarterly count of S&P 500 earnings calls citing "AI" · quarterly · private data. Confounders: hype; mentions are not actions; counts the term "AI" only, not headcount language. Compared with: FactSet's 5-year and 10-year averages of calls citing AI.
  • AI's share of US venture deal value. PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor (quarterly report) · quarterly · private data. Confounders: the funding cycle; a few megarounds (OpenAI, Anthropic) dominate the share; AI tagging differs by data provider.
  • AI's share of global venture funding. Crunchbase News quarterly global funding reports (Crunchbase data) · quarterly · private data. Confounders: the funding cycle; a few megarounds (OpenAI, Anthropic) dominate the share; AI tagging differs by data provider.
  • Long-run real interest rate: 10-year TIPS yield (strong-form test only, not required for any mark). Federal Reserve Board, H.15 Selected Interest Rates (10-year inflation-indexed Treasury constant maturity), via FRED · daily · official data. Confounders: monetary policy; deficits; the inflation risk premium.

History

  • 2026-10-06 Start → No clear sign: J1 starts at No clear sign: its Emerging mark ("one primary indicator, either the basket derating against the comparison beyond what its earnings changes explain, or positive market reactions to AI-linked headcount cuts at several named firms within a quarter.") is not met. Since 2021 the three Nasdaq indexes for labor-priced industries have fallen far behind the broad market and sit below their 2015-2021 ranges against it, and capital flows heavily to AI: 86% of US venture deal value in the first half of 2026, and hyperscaler capex up about 80%. The Emerging tests are still not met. These price-only indexes cannot separate a derating from earnings changes, and 2026 is mixed: the employment-agency and computer-services indexes have roughly kept pace with the market this year. On market reactions to AI-linked cuts we have one clear positive case, Block, whose shares rose more than 20% after hours, against Goldman Sachs research that found share prices fell about 2% on average after layoff announcements.

J2 · Firms cut hiring for AI before AI can do the whole job

No clear sign

Companies are freezing or shrinking headcount and naming AI as the reason, while AI still can't do the whole job; output per worker rises.

Why we track it: this is how capital's expectation (J1) becomes a labor-market fact.

Marks

  • Established: Supported for two quarters, visible in official industry data against the comparison, with revenue per employee rising at the pinned firms and AI-washing examined and ruled out.
  • Supported: firm evidence (statements with headcount or seat data) and an official series (JOLTS comparison or offshore headcount) agree across two releases.
  • Emerging: one primary indicator, either repeated named statements from large firms tying cuts to AI with headcount data confirming the cuts, or the exposed industries' JOLTS hires rate falling below the comparison group beyond its pre-2022 range.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: firms that cited AI reverse or rehire and exposed-industry hiring keeps pace with the comparison, in two sources across two releases.

Indicators

  • Named company statements tying hiring freezes or cuts to AI. Company statements: CEO memos, earnings calls and SEC filings (EDGAR full-text search for filings) · event-driven · private data. Confounders: AI-washing: AI named as cover for cuts made for cost or over-hiring reasons.
  • Job cuts attributed to AI (Challenger, Gray & Christmas monthly job-cut report). Challenger, Gray & Christmas, The Challenger Report · monthly · private data. Confounders: the reason is self-reported by employers; announced cuts, not completed layoffs. Compared with: all announced job cuts (AI's share of the total).
  • WARN layoff notices (state labor departments). State WARN notices: New York State DOL WARN Dashboard (asks whether 'technological innovation or automation' caused the layoff); California EDD WARN reports · continuous · official data. Confounders: notices rarely give a reason (New York's automation question is the exception); WARN thresholds miss small and remote cuts.
  • Revenue per employee at the pinned revenuePerEmployee firms. SEC filings (10-K, 10-Q, 20-F; XBRL company facts where available); for non-SEC filers (TCS, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra, Teleperformance) their own annual reports · quarterly or annual · official data. Confounders: price increases; acquisitions and divestitures; outsourcing and contractors outside headcount. Compared with: each firm's own pre-2022 trend.
  • Seat and retention disclosures at the pinned seatVendors, paired with hyperscalers' disclosed token volumes. Company filings and earnings releases of the seatVendors (paid seats, net revenue retention, renewal and attrition, comments on customer headcount) and of Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon (token or inference volumes) · quarterly · official data. Confounders: vendors moving from per-seat to per-agent and per-outcome pricing; downturn spending cuts and trimming of unused seats; vendor consolidation.
  • Quarterly headcount at the pinned offshoreServices firms. Company results and annual reports: TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech, Tech Mahindra (quarterly); Concentrix and Teleperformance (annual reports) · quarterly (Indian IT firms); annual (Concentrix, Teleperformance) · official data. Confounders: demand in client industries; pricing pressure; visa and trade policy.
  • Philippine IT-BPM industry headcount. IT and Business Process Association of the Philippines (IBPAP), annual industry figures · annual · private data. Confounders: an industry body's own estimates and targets; demand in client industries; trade and outsourcing policy.
  • JOLTS hires rate and quits rate, tracked separately, for Information and Professional and business services against less-exposed industries. U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (via FRED; Information via BLS) · monthly (read as 3-month averages) · official data. Confounders: the rate cycle (handled by the comparison); sample noise; revisions. Compared with: Construction, Health care and social assistance, Leisure and hospitality (same seasonally adjusted JOLTS rates).
  • Freelance platforms: Upwork and Fiverr filings and academic studies on platform data. Upwork and Fiverr quarterly results and SEC filings (active clients or buyers, spend per client, category mix); academic studies using platform data · quarterly (filings); irregular (studies) · official data. Confounders: post-pandemic normalization; both platforms remaking themselves as AI companies.

History

  • 2026-10-06 Start → No clear sign: J2 starts at No clear sign: its Emerging mark ("one primary indicator, either repeated named statements from large firms tying cuts to AI with headcount data confirming the cuts, or the exposed industries' JOLTS hires rate falling below the comparison group beyond its pre-2022 range.") is not met. Firms are naming AI. Block cut its workforce by more than 40%, saying a smaller team using AI tools can do more, and its quarterly report records the cut. Amazon tied its October 2025 round to AI, and Challenger counted 120,136 announced US cuts attributed to AI in January-September 2026. The Emerging mark is not met: Block is the only large firm whose AI-linked cut is confirmed by headcount data, Amazon framed its January round as removing bureaucracy, and the Information hires rate reached the bottom edge of its 2017-2019 range against the comparison industries only in August, after running ahead of them from January to June. AI-washing and the unwinding of 2021-22 over-hiring remain live explanations, and the Yale, New York Fed, OECD and Census studies find no broad AI footprint in hiring.

J3 · Entry-level knowledge work goes first

Emerging

The first jobs AI displaces are entry-level knowledge-work jobs: young workers in AI-exposed occupations lose ground to their peers in less-exposed work, and firms shrink graduate intake.

Why we track it: entry-level work is the most automatable part of most knowledge jobs, and cutting juniors is also a bet: a firm that stops hiring juniors is betting it won't need as many seniors in about ten years.

Marks

  • Established: Supported for two quarters, visible in BLS data, with the comparison group ruling out the rate cycle.
  • Supported: two independent datasets (payroll research and postings, or the New York Fed series) show it across two releases.
  • Emerging: one primary dataset shows young workers in exposed jobs falling relative to the comparison group, beyond the pre-2022 range.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: young workers in exposed jobs keep pace with the comparison group in two sources across two releases.

Indicators

  • Employment of 22-25-year-olds in AI-exposed against less-exposed occupations (ADP payroll research). Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, 'Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence' (ADP payroll data) · irregular (paper revisions) · private data. Confounders: the rate cycle; the unwinding of 2021-22 tech over-hiring. Compared with: workers aged 22-25 in less-exposed occupations, and older workers in the same occupations.
  • Recent-graduate unemployment against all workers. Federal Reserve Bank of New York, The Labor Market for Recent College Graduates (Census CPS data) · quarterly · official data. Confounders: the rate cycle; the unwinding of 2021-22 tech over-hiring. Compared with: all workers aged 22-65 and all young workers.
  • Junior postings and postings in exposed against less-exposed occupational sectors (Indeed). Indeed Hiring Lab, Job Postings Index by occupational sector (open data on GitHub); junior and entry-level postings from Hiring Lab reports · daily data refreshed weekly; irregular reports · private data. Confounders: postings are not hires; job-board coverage changes; the index starts 2020-02-01, so the pre-2022 baseline is short. Compared with: less-exposed sectors in the same data (e.g. Nursing, Construction, Personal Care & Home Health, Food Preparation & Service).
  • Graduate intake at the Big Four and large law firms. Firm announcements (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC); NALP research on law-firm recruiting and new-graduate employment · annual · private data. Confounders: demand for audit and legal work; offshoring.
  • College majors: computer science and other fields against the pre-2022 trend. National Student Clearinghouse Research Center, Final Fall Enrollment Trends and Final Spring Enrollment Trends (major-field data appendix) · twice a year (fall report in January, spring report mid-year) · private data. Confounders: counter-cyclical moves into school in a weak job market; new majors (data science, AI) absorbing computer science. Compared with: each field's pre-2022 trend.
  • Computing degree production and enrollment (CRA Taulbee Survey). Computing Research Association, CRA Taulbee Survey · annual (published each May) · private data. Confounders: covers only PhD-granting departments; counter-cyclical enrollment. Compared with: pre-2022 trend.
  • Law school applicant volume (LSAC). Law School Admission Council, Current Volume Summaries · daily during the admission cycle (read weekly) · private data. Confounders: counter-cyclical moves into law school in a weak job market; LSAT and admission-cycle changes. Compared with: same point in earlier cycles, against the pre-2022 trend.
  • Bar exam takers (NCBE). National Conference of Bar Examiners, The Bar Examiner statistics (MBE, MPRE and annual bar admission statistics) · twice a year (February and July exams); annual statistics each spring · private data. Confounders: the NextGen bar exam transition; counter-cyclical law school enrollment. Compared with: pre-2022 trend.
  • NCLEX nursing licensure exam volumes (NCSBN). National Council of State Boards of Nursing, NCLEX pass rates dashboard (volume and pass rates by candidate type) · annual dashboards, updated during the year · private data. Confounders: the 2023 Next Generation NCLEX change; nursing school capacity limits; internationally educated candidates. Compared with: pre-2022 trend.
  • Accounting graduates and CPA Exam candidates (AICPA, NASBA). AICPA Trends Report (accounting graduates, new-graduate hiring, CPA Exam candidates); NASBA candidate data · annual · private data. Confounders: the CPA 150-hour rule and its new alternative pathways; the 2024 CPA Exam redesign. Compared with: pre-2022 trend.

History

  • 2026-10-06 Start → Emerging: J3 meets its Emerging mark ("one primary dataset shows young workers in exposed jobs falling relative to the comparison group, beyond the pre-2022 range."); its Supported mark ("two independent datasets (payroll research and postings, or the New York Fed series) show it across two releases.") is not met. In ADP payroll data, Stanford researchers find that employment of 22-25-year-olds in highly AI-exposed occupations was about 19% below the path of their peers in less-exposed occupations as of June 2026, up from 15% a year earlier, with no such gap for experienced workers. They report that the gap holds when tech firms are excluded and exposure to interest rates and remote work is controlled, which covers the claim's named confounders. Supported is not met. Dallas Fed work on Census CPS data points the same way but ends in September 2025, and the New York Fed's recent-graduate unemployment gap sits only at or slightly above its pre-2022 high. Against the claim, New York Fed postings research finds that the decline in exposed occupations began before ChatGPT and is not concentrated in entry-level jobs, a Warwick paper attributes most of the fall in junior hiring to work-from-home exposure, and the Stanford authors say their estimates weaken with education controls and are descriptive, not causal.

J4 · Value moves to the physical bottlenecks

Established

As cognition gets cheap, the scarce physical inputs (electric power, grid capacity, chips, land with power and water, and materials such as copper) capture a growing share of the value, and their owners gain pricing power.

Why we track it: if cognitive labor stops being the bottleneck, the next bottlenecks are physical, and capital is already moving there.

Marks

  • Established: Supported for two quarters in official data, with bottleneck owners' margins rising beyond inflation.
  • Supported: prices or auctions and lead times or margins agree across two releases.
  • Emerging: one primary indicator, for example capacity or retail power prices in data-center regions rising faster than in comparison regions, attributed by the grid operator or regulator to data-center load.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: bottleneck prices and lead times ease as supply catches up and margins compress, in two sources across two releases.

Indicators

  • Data-center power deals: purchase agreements, nuclear restarts and new plants tied to AI load. Power producers' and hyperscalers' 8-K and other SEC filings (EDGAR full-text search), plus the companies' own announcements · event-driven · official data. Confounders: deals announced but not built; electrification and reshoring load unrelated to AI; contracts re-labelled from earlier plans.
  • PJM capacity auction clearing prices (Base Residual Auction). PJM Interconnection, Capacity Market (RPM): Base Residual Auction reports and results · annual (one Base Residual Auction per delivery year), plus planning parameters before each · official data. Confounders: generator retirements; auction rule changes and the price cap and floor; natural gas prices. Compared with: PJM's pre-2022 auctions, and capacity prices in regions without data-center clusters.
  • Large-load tariffs for data centers approved or proposed by state utility commissions. State utility commission dockets, indexed by DELTa (Database of Emerging Large-Load Tariffs), Smart Electric Power Alliance and NC Clean Energy Technology Center · event-driven; DELTa updated quarterly · official data. Confounders: tariffs written to shield households rather than to price scarcity; proposed tariffs that are never approved.
  • Interconnection queue volume and time from request to commercial operation. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up: Characteristics of Power Plants Seeking Transmission Interconnection (with GridTracker; data from all seven ISOs/RTOs and 50 non-ISO utilities) · annual · official data. Confounders: speculative requests that inflate queues; FERC Order 2023 queue reforms; solar and storage policy changes. Compared with: earlier editions of the same report (pre-2022 queue durations).
  • Gas turbine backlog and slot reservations against annual output. GE Vernova quarterly results, 10-Q and earnings releases (Gas Power equipment backlog and slot reservation agreements, GW) · quarterly · official data. Confounders: reservations that are not firm orders; gas demand for reasons other than data centers; one maker's share shifting. Compared with: the maker's stated annual production capacity (backlog in years of output).
  • Transformer lead times and supply shortfall. Wood Mackenzie supply-chain research on US power and distribution transformers · irregular · private data. Confounders: tariffs and import restrictions on equipment; replacement of ageing transformers; copper and electrical-steel input costs. Compared with: 2021 lead times (about 50 weeks for power transformers, per Wood Mackenzie).
  • Producer prices of power and distribution transformers (a price proxy for lead times, not a lead-time measure). BLS Producer Price Index by Commodity: Power and Distribution Transformers, Except Parts, via FRED · monthly · official data. Confounders: copper and electrical-steel prices; tariffs on imported equipment; general producer inflation. Compared with: PPI all commodities (fred:PPIACO).
  • Retail electricity prices by state: data-center states against the US average. U.S. Energy Information Administration, Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A (average price to ultimate customers by sector, by state) · monthly · official data. Confounders: natural gas prices; weather; transmission and wildfire costs unrelated to data centers. Compared with: states without data-center clusters, and the US average.
  • US city average retail price of electricity per kWh against overall inflation. BLS average price data (CPI program), via FRED · monthly · official data. Confounders: natural gas prices; weather; general inflation. Compared with: CPI all items (fred:CPIAUCSL).
  • Global price of copper. IMF Primary Commodity Prices, via FRED · monthly · official data. Confounders: tariffs on metals; China's construction cycle; electrification unrelated to AI (vehicles, grids). Compared with: IMF all-commodities price index (fred:PALLFNFINDEXM).
  • Chip foundry gross margin. TSMC quarterly results (earnings release and management report) · quarterly · official data. Confounders: capacity utilization swings; currency moves; overseas fab cost dilution. Compared with: TSMC's own pre-2022 gross margins.
  • Data-center vacancy and rents in primary North American markets. CBRE, North America Data Center Trends · semiannual · private data. Confounders: construction delays from power and permitting; broker survey coverage. Compared with: pre-2022 vacancy and rents in the same markets.
  • Utilities' authorized return on equity in rate cases. S&P Global Market Intelligence, Regulatory Research Associates (RRA) rate case summaries · semiannual · private data. Confounders: interest rates; rider cases mixed with general rate cases; state regulatory climate. Compared with: the 10-year Treasury yield (fred:DGS10).

PJM's 2028/29 capacity auction cleared at its $325 per MW-day cap, after $333.44 for 2027/28 and $329.17 for 2026/27, against $76.53-164.77 in the 2018/19-2021/22 auctions. J5 · The human moats hold, for now

No clear sign

Work protected by dexterity in messy physical settings, by legal accountability (a human who signs and can be held liable) or by the value of human presence keeps its jobs and pay while AI-exposed work weakens.

Why we track it: these are the jobs that are supposed to be left. "For now" is the point: the tracker watches the clocks that would end each moat.

Marks

  • Established: Supported for two quarters in official data, with no scaled substitution in the robot clock.
  • Supported: pay or employment and the robot clock agree (protected work holding while deployments stay at pilot scale) across two releases.
  • Emerging: one official series shows protected work's pay or employment beating the AI-exposed comparison beyond the pre-2022 range.
  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
  • Contradicted: scaled robot deployments in trades or driving, or removal of human-signature requirements, with protected occupations' employment or pay falling against the comparison, in two sources across two releases.

Indicators

  • Pay in the trades against all private-sector workers; plus specialty trade contractors (BLS). U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Employment Statistics: average hourly earnings, construction and total private (via FRED) · monthly · official data. Confounders: the construction cycle; immigration policy. Compared with: total private average hourly earnings (all employees from 2006; the production and nonsupervisory pair, from 1947 and 1964, gives the longer pre-2022 range).
  • Registered apprenticeships. Emerging

  • No clear sign: The indicators are flat or mixed, stay inside their pre-2022 range, or a named confounder fully explains them. This is the default when no other mark is met.
