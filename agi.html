The test, the evidence and who is working on it

Question. Can one AI system do at least four-fifths of the paid work people do at a computer (design, analysis, writing, law, finance, media, engineering), rather than a few kinds of task?

Where it stands. Claude Opus 5.5 delivers 21.25% of real freelance projects at client-acceptable quality on the Remote Labor Index (GPT-6 Astra: 20.83%), against our 80%. On one-shot, well-specified GDPval tasks, lab-reported overall scores have been above 80% since March 2026, but no results by occupation are published. The gap between the two measures is the main finding.

  • ?Not measured: GDPval wins or ties 80%+ in at least 36 of 44 occupations (84.9% overall (GPT-5.5); no per-occupation results published)

Measure. Primary: the Remote Labor Index (RLI; Scale AI and CAIS) automation rate: the share of 240 real paid freelance projects (23 of Upwork's 64 work categories; median 11.5 hours of human work, mean 28.9) where human evaluators judge the AI's deliverable at least as good as the professional's, so that a reasonable client would accept it. Cross-check: OpenAI's GDPval 'wins or ties' rate against professionals' own deliverables (44 occupations; tasks average about 7 hours of expert work), broken down by occupation as in the GDPval paper. Context only, not tests: LLM-judged reruns of GDPval (Artificial Analysis, Mercor), which don't compare against the professionals; the Anthropic Economic Index, which measures use; and O*NET exposure studies, which measure potential exposure.

Marks. Partial: Remote Labor Index (RLI) 40% or more, or the GDPval per-occupation half passed. Close: RLI 60% or more. Met: RLI 80% or more, and on GDPval wins or ties in 80% or more of comparisons in at least 36 of the 44 occupations.

Details. Met when the best public system reaches an RLI automation rate of 80% or more and, on GDPval, wins or ties in 80% or more of comparisons in at least 36 of the 44 occupations (80% of them), not only on the overall average. No per-occupation GDPval results are published for current models, so that half is unmeasured today. Reliability (repeat success) and multi-week length are scored by their own components.

Why this test. The breadth clause asks for a share of real economic work, so the test should be a share of real work. RLI is the closest public match: whole paid projects, judged against what a client would accept. GDPval checks quality against experienced professionals (its task writers averaged 14 years' experience) but uses one-shot, well-specified tasks, so it overstates breadth. Neither samples the whole remote economy: GDPval covers 44 occupations in 9 industries, and RLI covers 23 freelance categories and leaves out work that needs client interaction or long-term evaluation. We'd switch to a benchmark that samples remote O*NET tasks by wage share if one is published.

Hard parts (6)

  • Whole projects aren't single deliverables. On one-shot, well-specified GDPval tasks, lab-reported scores are above 80%, but the best system still fails about four in five real freelance projects on the RLI. In the RLI paper's analysis of the launch agents' deliverables, 45.6% were poor quality, 35.7% were incomplete, 17.6% had corrupt or unusable files and 14.8% had inconsistencies (one deliverable can count in several), for example videos of 8 seconds where 8 minutes were requested, and floor plans that didn't match the supplied sketches. verified fact source
  • Much remote work produces files other than text: 3D and CAD models, architecture drawings, video, audio and design files. The RLI includes them. Scale reports that the most common failure is amateur-quality work, while successes cluster in audio work, image generation (logos, ads), report writing and data retrieval. The RLI still leaves out work that needs direct client interaction, physical labor or long-term evaluation, and covers 23 of Upwork's 64 categories. verified fact source
  • GDPval is one-shot and well specified. OpenAI says it doesn't capture building context, improving over multiple drafts or navigating ambiguity, such as talking to a client before deciding what to produce. OpenAI said in September 2025 that future versions would be more interactive; we have found none published. verified fact (part our inference) source
  • LLM judges overrate newer models, and the most often updated GDPval reruns use them. CAIS calibrated an automated judge on earlier models and found it overestimated GPT-5.5's RLI rate about 2.9 times (17.9% against 6.25% from human evaluators) and Claude Opus 4.8's about 2.3 times (18.8% against 8.33%), although it ranked the models correctly. Artificial Analysis's GDPval-AA and Mercor's GDPval leaderboards are LLM-judged and don't compare with the professionals' deliverables. The rankings only partly agree: Claude Opus 5.5 leads both the RLI and GDPval-AA, but GPT-6 Astra, second on the RLI, is 37th on GDPval-AA. verified fact source
  • New tasks score lower than the public ones. Mercor's GDPval Extended adds 132 tasks it wrote to the 213 public GDPval tasks: Claude Opus 5.5 (Max) scores 88.3% on the public tasks and 75.8% on the extended benchmark, and GPT-6 Astra (Max) 86.7% and 64.1%. These are LLM-judged mean rubric scores, not wins or ties against professionals. verified fact source
  • Deployment evidence leans on coding. Tasks tied to Computer and Mathematical occupations were 35% of Claude.ai conversations in February 2026, and more coding is moving to Anthropic's API through Claude Code, although Anthropic reports that Claude.ai use cases diversified (the top 10 tasks fell from 24% to 19% of traffic). The index measures what people bring to Claude, not what it can do. verified fact source

Could it be hidden?

Breadth is hard to observe for an undisclosed system. RLI scores exist only for systems Scale and CAIS can run (public releases, or models a lab submits), and GDPval win rates only for systems a lab chooses to report, so a hidden system passing this component would leave no trace on the leaderboards (A). It would more likely show up indirectly. The first route is economic footprint, which is hypothesis C's sanctioned path: the work gets sold. Signs would be an unexplained surge of high-quality remote deliverables on freelance platforms, or a sharp drop in hiring for outsourced design, analysis and media work. The second is a lab's own productivity claims: Anthropic's prototype R&D Automation Index says Claude 'leads' 26% of its AI R&D work as of August 2026, a measure that tracks A and B better than breadth. The third is a jump in a public model's RLI score after long internal use, which would show how far behind disclosure runs. Breadth on its own isn't evidence for B. For D, broad advisory work in government would show up through disclosed contracts and use (D-open), not through these benchmarks.

Question. When the system hands in a piece of work (a memo, a spreadsheet model, a legal draft, a design), experienced people in that job judge it at least as good as what a typical skilled professional would have delivered.

Where it stands. The well-specified half is cleared in aggregate only: GPT-5.5 wins or ties industry professionals in 84.9% of GDPval comparisons (OpenAI, April 2026), against our 50%, with no results by occupation. The real-project half is not: the best Remote Labor Index rate is 21.25% (Claude Opus 5.5) against 50%, and the best all-pass score on Harvey's legal benchmark is 19.5%.

Measure. Human grading of the system's deliverable against a professional's deliverable for the same task. Two parts: (1) well-specified tasks: blind head-to-head grading by experts from the same occupation, as the rate of wins or ties against professionals (GDPval or a successor), reported per occupation; (2) real end-to-end projects: the share of real commissioned projects where human evaluators, taking a reasonable client's view, judge the system's deliverable at least as good as the professional's (Remote Labor Index automation rate). All-pass legal rubrics (Harvey LAB, applied by an LLM judge to attorney-written criteria) are a domain check only.

Marks. Partial: either half passed, or RLI 25% or more. Close: RLI 35% or more with the well-specified half passed by occupation. Met: (a) expert-graded wins or ties 50%+, with at least as many wins as losses where wins are reported, on well-specified tasks in at least 80% of occupations, and (b) RLI 50% or more, held across two leaderboard updates.

Details. Met when, under a general harness, the best public system (a) wins or ties the professional at least 50% of the time, with at least as many wins as losses where wins are reported, on expert-graded well-specified tasks in at least 80% of occupations tested, and (b) has its deliverable judged at least as good as the professional's on at least 50% of real end-to-end projects (RLI automation rate 50% or more). 50% is parity: the system's work matches or beats the professional's on half the tasks; with comparison professionals who are typical or better, that is at least a median professional's level (our inference).

Why this test. The definition asks for the level of a median skilled professional, so the natural measure is a comparison with professionals' own deliverables, judged by people. Part (a) alone is not enough: GDPval's own authors say its tasks are precisely specified and one-shot, while real work involves ambiguity, iteration and long deliverables; part (b) tests quality on real projects. GDPval's comparison workers average 14 years of experience, above the median, so parity with them is a conservative test (our inference). Wins-or-ties alone would let a model that loses more often than it wins pass when ties are common, hence the wins-versus-losses check. LLM-judged scores do not count toward the threshold: CAIS found an automated judge overstated RLI rates two to three times for the newest models. Exam scores (bar, medical licensing) are excluded as the main test because they measure knowledge, not work product, and have overstated professional competence before. How many occupations and how reliably are left to the breadth and reliability components.

Why this test. The definition asks for 80%-or-better success on at least 80% of tasks, including multi-week projects. Pass^4 is the share of tasks done right on all four tries. With four tries it is the natural estimate of the share of tasks done at least 80% of the time (three of four is only 75%), and an average over lucky and unlucky runs cannot pass it. Could it be hidden?

Reasoning is the component most likely to show if a hidden system has it, because its products are checkable and valuable: a solved open problem, a Lean proof, an unusual run of papers. So far labs have claimed such results openly, often from unreleased models: OpenAI's Erdős unit-distance disproof (May 20), Navier–Stokes proof (Sept 8) and Oct 6 release of 722 manuscripts from one unreleased model (Epoch counts one as an AI-alone solve), and Anthropic's Riemann-zeros bound (Aug 10). That shows internal reasoning runs ahead of public models (relevant to A), but because the results were disclosed they are not evidence of secrecy; Epoch notes that the most impressive AI math results have come from inside AI companies with little transparency about how they were obtained. Attribution is already loose: Epoch marks the Sept 17 proof that ζ(5) is irrational 'human + AI' with an 'unknown AI system', though the preprint does not acknowledge particularly substantial AI use. So one unattributed solution would not point to a hidden system; a run of strong solutions to listed problems (Epoch's lists, erdosproblems.com) or Lean submissions with no stated author or model would matter for C. It would not show if the owner keeps results private: a government program (A) or a system advising decision-makers (D) leaves no published trace, and Epoch says only OpenAI has bought its Open Problems verifiers, so private checking is possible. Mathematical reasoning says little directly about B; AI R&D share is the better gauge there (our inference).

Why this test. The 80% success rate is the definition's "reliably (80% or better)", and human-hours of work are the most direct published measure of "multi-week projects"; a work-month covers projects of two to four weeks. It misses messiness: METR describes its tasks as mostly software, machine-learning and cybersecurity work, self-contained and well specified, and both METR and MirrorCode's authors find far longer horizons where a precise, checkable target exists. That is why checkable-only results don't count by themselves and why we require a documented real project for 'met'. The status is the same with a looser two-week (80-hour) bar.

Question. Can the AI be handed a computer, ordinary tools and a goal, and finish real work on its own, without people building a custom pipeline for each job or stepping in along the way?

Where it stands. Hands-off success clears 80% only on short tasks: 86% on OSWorld-Verified's few-minute desktop tasks (Claude Fable 5). Measure. Strict (full-completion) success, pass@1, on long-horizon professional computer workflows run on one general harness for every task: a virtual machine, the model's standard computer-use and shell tools, a written goal, a step budget, and no per-task pipelines, hints or mid-task human input. Primary benchmark: the official OSWorld 2.x leaderboard's 'Foundation E2E GUI' track (108 workflows, a median of about 1.6 hours for a skilled human, 500-step cap). Cross-check in real use: the share of all delegated tasks that succeed with no human intervention, by task length, as published by a lab or evaluator (today only OpenAI publishes this).

Marks. Partial: OSWorld 2.x 50% strict on the official general harness, or either part passed. Close: 65% strict, or 80% of 4–8 hour tasks done with no human intervention. Met: 80% strict, and 80% of multi-day tasks (16 h or more) done with no human intervention.

Details. Met when a public system scores at least 80% strict on OSWorld 2.x under the official harness, and a lab or evaluator reports that at least 80% of multi-day tasks (16 or more hours of human work) succeed with no human intervention. We mark it close at 65% strict, or when at least 80% of 4-8 hour tasks succeed with no intervention.

Why this test. 80% is the definition's own reliability bar, one general harness with no mid-task help is its 'no task-specific human scaffolding' clause, and only a fully finished task counts, because partial credit is not what a client accepts. We count hands-off successes as a share of all tasks, not of successful ones, so a system can't pass by rarely succeeding but doing it unaided. What it misses: OSWorld tasks last hours, not weeks (the long-horizon component covers that); OSWorld 2.x publishes no human success rate, so we can't say whether skilled people would clear 80% strict (OSWorld 1.0's human baseline was 72.36%); and the only intervention data are OpenAI's own, from interactive research-coding sessions, judged by its own classifier (checked against people on 25 tasks), on tasks researchers chose to delegate.

Measure. A two-part proxy, run with a general harness only (no per-task fine-tuning, no task-specific scaffolding). (1) Learning from experience: normalized gain on Continual Learning Bench (CL-Bench, from UC Berkeley, UW-Madison and Snorkel AI). This is the share of the available improvement a system captures when it carries what it learned through a sequence of related tasks, compared with the same system starting each task fresh, in six expert-validated domains from codebases to demand forecasting. (2) Learning from documents: MTOB-style tasks. The system learns a skill it cannot have seen in training (a new language, tool or codebase) only from the material a human learner would get, and is scored against a human who learned from the same material. Retention is checked by re-testing after a long run of unrelated work.

Marks. Provisional. Partial: CL-Bench normalized gain 30% or more, or any one of the three parts passed. Close: CL-Bench 40% or more. Met: all three parts: CL-Bench 50% or more with positive gain in 80% of domain runs; the human learner's level on a task released after the model's training cutoff (for a language, in both translation directions); 90% of the gain kept after unrelated work.

Details. Provisional, our inference. (1) An average normalized gain of 50% or more across CL-Bench's six domains, with positive gain in at least 80% of domain runs. (2) A score at or above the human learner on an MTOB-style task released after the model's training cutoff (for a language, in both translation directions). (3) At least 90% of the learned gain still present after the unrelated work. We will replace the 50% with a measured median-professional learning curve as soon as a benchmark publishes one.

Why this test. The definition says the system learns without task-specific retraining. Our working component adds that it keeps what it learns, at roughly a professional's pace. CL-Bench separates learning from baseline skill by comparing stateful and stateless runs of the same system; its authors call it the first benchmark to do this across diverse real-world domains. MTOB is the best-known test of learning a skill from documents alone. What the proxy misses: CL-Bench has no human baseline yet, so 'professional pace' is not measured. Its sequences run 12 to 120 task instances, far short of the months a new hire spends, and its first round tested only memory kept in context, retrieval or notes, not weight updates such as test-time training. It cannot tell learning in the weights from notes kept in a memory file; we count both, since the definition only bars task-specific retraining. On MTOB, most of the gain comes from the book's example sentences rather than its grammar explanations. Tacit and social knowledge, such as how a particular team works, is not tested, and MTOB measures one skill learned in one sitting.

Hard parts (7)

  • Learning in the weights is hard. Google Research says today's models know only what is in their context window or what they learned in pre-training, and that updating a model's parameters with new data often causes catastrophic forgetting of old skills. Its Nested Learning work (NeurIPS 2025) targets this problem. verified fact source
  • Notes and memory do not yet add up to skill. On CL-Bench, agents often overfit to their latest observations or fail to reuse what they learned across instances, what an agent accumulates often hurts rather than helps, and plain in-context learning beats dedicated memory systems on most tasks. verified fact source
  • Learning from its own feedback makes an agent drift. SkillLearnBench (Carnegie Mellon and Amazon AGI) finds every skill-learning method beats having no skills, but none leads across all tasks and models; repeated rounds help when feedback comes from outside, while self-feedback alone causes recursive drift. verified fact source
  • Learning from documents may be narrower than it looks. A study of MTOB finds almost all of the translation gain comes from the grammar book's parallel example sentences, not its grammar explanations, and no evidence that long-context models use the explanations effectively (Aycock and colleagues, ICLR 2025). verified fact source
  • There is a measurement gap. No benchmark we found has a human learning-pace baseline (Snorkel says it is working on one for some CL-Bench tasks), and the newest models (Opus 5.5, Fable 5.1, Astra, Argon) are not on CL-Bench, so 'professional pace' cannot be checked yet. our inference source
  • Picking up a workplace's unwritten standards is still weak. Given as much reference material as a new hire would get, GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 'consistently missed implicit conventions that humans would pick up', such as Epoch's visual style and what its audience cares about. verified fact source
  • Gains from experience vary and carry over only partly. In an unreviewed preprint, researchers at Meta Superintelligence Labs, UC Berkeley, Princeton and the University of Washington measure 'agent plasticity', how efficiently agents turn experience into reusable artifacts and gains, and find frontier models' improvement trajectories differ sharply, while gains often transfer only partially out of distribution. expert opinion source

Could it be hidden?

Our inference. A hidden AGI (A) would need this component more than any other, and it is where public systems are weakest across tasks: 25.4% of the available gain on CL-Bench. Passing it in secret would therefore take a large unpublished jump. The component also ties to B and C. A system that keeps learning from its own work is one route to recursive self-improvement (B). A system running unseen in the economy (C) would need to adapt to new clients and tools; an operator could get partway there with external memory files, which leave no public trace. Four signs could show it. (1) A lab's next release jumps far beyond its predecessor on CL-Bench or on a human-baselined learning evaluation. (2) A deployed model's scores on fixed evaluations rise between announced versions, which would suggest undisclosed updates from deployment; re-runs by Artificial Analysis or CL-Bench could catch this. (3) A lab stops publishing continual-learning work it had been publishing, such as Google's Nested Learning line, while it keeps hiring for it. (4) Agents in the economy visibly improve on a client's own workflow over weeks without a model change. We have not seen any of these. Learning in the weights inside a lab, used only internally, would be hard to see from outside until a release or a disclosure (A, B). D does not depend on this component directly.

The test, the evidence and who is working on it

Question. Can the system handle a kind of problem it has never seen before (new rules, a new game, a new field) about as well as a capable person working it out for the first time, not only the kinds of problems it was trained on?

Where it stands. Within three months of ARC-AGI-3's launch the best public score was 1.5%, against our 80%. Once the format was known, GPT-6 Astra reached 62.7% with the Standard harness (99.9% with the Provider Adapter) at about 27 to 37 times the paid human cost per game, and its lead did not carry over to WitnessBench's held-out games.

0 of 2 parts of the test met.

  • –Not yet: 80%+ on a new ARC generation within 3 months, Standard harness, at most 10x human cost (1.5% within ARC-AGI-3's first 3 months)
  • –Not yet: The lead holds on an independent held-out suite (Astra's ARC-AGI-3 lead does not carry over to WitnessBench)

Measure. Two parts, both under a general harness (no game- or task-specific code, prompts or tools). (1) Fresh-benchmark test: the score on ARC Prize's newest benchmark generation (ARC-AGI-3 today, ARC-AGI-4 next) on its hidden Semi-Private set with ARC's Standard harness, counting only models released before or within 3 months of the benchmark's public release. We show ARC's Provider Adapter results alongside: that harness adds the provider's own general context settings, not task-specific help, so whether it counts is the owner's call. (2) Transfer check: the same model's lead must hold, within error, on at least one independent held-out suite of the same kind that labs could not have trained on (today, WitnessBench's private test games), so a score cannot come from training on the genre alone.

Marks. Partial: a public system passes 50% on the newest ARC generation only after it has been public for months, or reaches 80% but fails the transfer check. Close: a model released before a new generation scores 50% or more at its launch. Met: 80% or more within 3 months of a new generation's launch, with the Standard harness, at no more than 10 times ARC's paid human cost, and the lead holds on an independent held-out suite.

Details. Met when, within 3 months of a new ARC generation's release, a public system scores at least 80% (our reliability bar) with the Standard harness, at no more than 10 times ARC's paid human cost per task (about $12.78 per attempted ARC-AGI-3 game, so about $128), and its lead holds on an independent held-out suite. Close: a model released before the new generation scores 50% or more at launch. Partial: systems pass 50% on the newest generation only after it has been public for months, or fail the transfer check. Far: below 50% even then. ARC scores 100% as beating every level of every game in no more actions than the median person who completed that level; on ARC-AGI-3's 25 public games 42% of first-time human plays were full solves, so we read 80% as a skilled first-time player, above a typical one (our inference; we found no published per-person score spread). ARC-AGI-3's 3-month window closed on June 25, 2026, so the earliest this can be met is ARC-AGI-4.

Why this test. Each ARC generation went from near 0% to near saturation once labs could aim at it: ARC-AGI-1 in about 5 years (87.5% at 172 times the standard compute, Dec 2024), ARC-AGI-2 in about 13 months to 85%, ARC-AGI-3 in about 5 months to 99.9% with the Provider Adapter harness. So only a score on tasks that are new to the system says much about generalization rather than preparation. ARC Prize's own view is that AGI arrives when tasks easy for people and hard for AI can no longer be built; this test applies that one generation at a time. What it misses: ARC games are short, closed, deterministic puzzles that use only core-knowledge priors, and ARC Prize says ARC-AGI-3 'does not represent the complexity and open-endedness of the real world'. No public, contamination-controlled test of transfer to genuinely new professional fields exists, so we treat that part as unmeasured and say so.

Also. Transfer check fails: on WitnessBench's 17 held-out games the best of 18 models clears about 5 of 20 levels per game. verified fact source

Could it be hidden?

A hidden system that generalized at skilled-human level would be hard to see in public benchmarks. ARC Prize tests models at its own discretion, mostly public ones, and publishes an unreleased model's result no later than 30 days after public release, so a model that is never released need never appear (testing policy). There is precedent: ARC Prize scored OpenAI's unreleased o3-preview in December 2024. OpenAI says an unreleased internal model is 'significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra' (Sept 8), and no ARC or other outside evaluation of it is listed. In March Chollet called ARC-AGI-3 an 'early warning signal': 'any sudden score jump will mean something important has changed about AI capabilities'. Observable traces: (A) a lab or ARC Prize disclosing a first-contact score on ARC-AGI-4 or a held-out suite well above the best public model, or a public model arriving with a near-human launch-day score, which would imply an internal predecessor had it earlier; (B) launch-to-saturation time shrinking from about 5 months toward weeks alongside lab claims of automated research, since fast adaptation to new tasks is what recursive self-improvement needs; (C) agents adapting covertly inside unfamiliar real systems beyond the sub-AGI incidents we track (so far all trace to sub-AGI agents or human operators); (D) only indirectly linked. Because labs can train toward any published genre, a strong public ARC score alone is not evidence of hidden AGI and a low one is not evidence against it; first-contact and held-out results are the signals we trust most.

What it leaves out, and why

Physical and embodied work (robotics, manual skills) is out of scope: we judge the mind, not the body. The main cognitive frameworks (Hendrycks et al., DeepMind's Levels of AGI, ARC) leave it out too, and v1.0 already counted remote work only, so leaving it out makes the bar no looser.

How v2.0 relates to v1.0

Through the reading of Oct 6, 2026, our bar was one test: It reliably (80% or better) does at least 80% of economically valuable remote professional tasks at the level of a median skilled professional, including multi-week projects, with no task-specific human scaffolding.
